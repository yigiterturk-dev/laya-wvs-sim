1. The survey pattern (no model involved)

For each country, respondents are split into thirds by a vulnerability score (low household income step, unemployment, low life satisfaction), using the country's own weighted terciles. Each row shows how much more often the most vulnerable third answers 7–10 on E037 ("the government should take more responsibility to ensure everyone is provided for") than the least vulnerable third, with a 95% respondent-bootstrap CI.

2. Does a bad national economy widen the gap?

The simulation below assumes a crisis. This section uses measured conditions instead. For each country the gap from section 1 is set against World Bank WDI API data averaged over the survey year and the 2 years before it. If hard times widened the divide, countries surveyed under high unemployment, high inflation or weak growth should show bigger gaps.

They do not. None of the three indicators shows a detectable relationship with the gap: every confidence interval spans zero and, across ~84 countries, the intervals exclude correlations stronger than about ±0.38. The divide between the vulnerable and the secure looks like a stable feature of how people relate to the state, one that tracks a household's own position more than the country's business cycle.

Pandemic fieldwork. Countries surveyed in 2020 or later show a mean gap of +8.1 pp (n = 25) against +9.2 pp before (n = 62); permutation p = 0.38.

Rank correlations, because inflation is heavily skewed (x-axis on a log scale). p-values from 5,000 permutations, CIs from a country bootstrap. This is a between-country comparison at one point in time; it cannot rule out that a given country's gap moves during its own crisis. No World Bank match: Northern Ireland, Taiwan.

3. What that structure does in a simulated crisis

Every country runs the same agent-based model: 5,000 agents sampled by survey weight, 10 rounds, 10 seeds. The crisis assumption ("drift", strength 0.6) nudges economically vulnerable agents toward "government responsible". The same model then runs on a placebo in which every attribute is shuffled independently, which keeps each variable's distribution and removes who-is-who. The structure effect is the real result minus the placebo result: the part of the simulated shift that comes from how vulnerability and attitudes actually co-occur in that country.

Correlation between the observed gap and the structure effect: -0.28. The structure effect is significantly negative in 9 of 87 countries and significantly positive in 4. This is a weak result. The expected ceiling effect (the vulnerable already lean toward state responsibility, so a crisis pushing them further has less room to act) points in the right direction, but in most countries the model cannot distinguish it from the placebo. In this model the mechanism, not the real data structure, drives most of the simulated shift.

Türkiye: simulated shift +0.064 [+0.057, +0.070] vs placebo +0.069; structure effect -0.005 [-0.013, +0.002] (mean stance on a −1…+1 scale).

4. How to read this

Section 1 is a descriptive survey finding. It shows association, not cause: vulnerability does not necessarily cause the attitude. Section 3 is a model output. It is conditional on an assumed crisis mechanism and is not a forecast.

  • Excluded because stance or income coverage is below 80%: Hungary, Italy, Portugal, Spain, North Macedonia.
  • Vulnerability is a proxy, not measured crisis exposure. Income steps are self-placed and country-relative.
  • Survey years differ by country (2017–2022), and some fieldwork fell during COVID-19.
  • The raw data stays local; only aggregates are published. Source: EVS_WVS_Joint_Csv_v5_0.csv, doi:10.14281/18241.21.
